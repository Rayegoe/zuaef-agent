# Scene-preserving writing

Method, not content. The material is the only source of scenes; this skill
tells you how to honor them.

## 1. Detect the flattening

Before revising, scan your draft for these tell-tale signs:

- Every paragraph ends in a judgment about society/people/时代.
- "他/她" appears only as the subject of "觉得/认为/需要" and never with
  verbs of action, pause, or speech.
- The words "其实/本质上/归根到底/现代人/当代" do a lot of the work.
- No paragraph contains a detail that could only have come from THIS material.

If the draft has these symptoms, it has stopped reporting and started
editorializing.

## 2. Return to the observed

- Re-read the material for scenes you skipped: what did the person do, say,
  wear, hold, wait for? What did the room look like? What happened in order?
- Restore the specific moment and let it carry the meaning. A person standing
  in a doorway at midnight says more than a sentence calling them lonely.
- Keep the sequence of action. Chronology inside a scene is a structure;
  do not compress it into a summary.

## 3. Let people act, not just feel

- Give the people in the piece verbs: they hold back, they check their phone,
  they repeat a phrase, they do not answer. Use verbs the material supports.
- Dialogue or quoted speech, when the material contains it, stays in the
  person's own words — do not smooth it into standard prose.
- Never invent an action, gesture or emotion the material does not contain.
  If you need a gesture, look for one in the material; if there is none, the
  absence is the fact.

## 4. One concrete anchor per paragraph

- Almost every paragraph should contain at least one concrete anchor: a
  number, a name, an object, a place, a verb with an object.
- Abstract sentences are allowed as connective tissue, but the paragraph
  must earn them with an anchor first.

## 5. Cut template judgment sentences

- "这就是XX的意义" / "或许，这就是城市夜生活的温度" — sterile formulations
  that mean nothing specific. Replace with what the material actually shows.
- Judgment belongs to the reader. The writer's job is to arrange the
  evidence so the judgment is available, not to pronounce it.
