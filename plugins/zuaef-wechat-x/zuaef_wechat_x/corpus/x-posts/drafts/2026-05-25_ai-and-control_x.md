1/7
AI does not give us control.
It reveals our addiction to control.

2/7
Most people believe better tools mean better command over reality.

More data.
More visibility.
More prediction.
More power.

But something strange happens: the more we try to manage everything, the less at ease we become.

3/7
Laozi gives us a useful lens for this.

"The movement of the Tao is by contraries."
— Tao Te Ching, §40

In other words: reality often pushes back against force.

Water does not defeat the rock by fighting it.
It flows around it until the rock is changed.

Wu-wei is not passivity.
It is the intelligence of not fighting the system you are part of.

4/7
Heidegger saw a similar danger in modern technology.

The essence of technology, he argued, is not the tool itself.

It is a way of seeing.

A forest becomes timber.
A river becomes energy.
A worker becomes labour.
Attention becomes a resource.

Everything becomes something to be ordered, optimised, and extracted.

5/7
AI intensifies this way of seeing.

The dashboard.
The prediction engine.
The recommendation system.
The productivity assistant.

They all whisper the same promise:

"What can be measured can be managed."

But the feeling of control is not the same as control.

Sometimes the dashboard is only a mirror showing us everything we have not yet optimised.

6/7
This is where the human question begins.

What happens when your sleep, diet, calendar, attention, emotions, and work are all treated as optimisation problems?

Where is the line between:

"I am using this tool"

and

"this tool is teaching me how to see myself"?

7/7
Epictetus wrote that people are disturbed not by things, but by the views they take of things.

Perhaps AI is not the disturbance.

Perhaps the disturbance is the belief that life should become fully manageable.

You are not outside the world, managing it.

You are in it.

Start there.
